Your dashboard can be correct. Your code can run perfectly. Your model can converge. And your answer can still be wrong. Imagine ten thousand reported records. Are they really ten thousand different people? The problem may have started before the analysis began.

Meet Amina, a synthetic person in our example. She registers at Clinic A, then moves and registers at Clinic B. One record says Amina K. Another uses capital letters. Her date of birth and phone number look different because the formats changed. Two records do not necessarily mean two people. Mobility, re-registration and disconnected systems can all create this problem.

Suppose adjudication establishes nine thousand two hundred unique people among ten thousand records. That leaves eight hundred extra records. Divide eight hundred by nine thousand two hundred: the apparent overcount is about eight point seven percent. The denominator matters. Inflated counts can distort coverage, resource allocation and downstream analysis. Correct arithmetic cannot repair a mistaken unit of counting.

Over the years, I have used a practical data-quality memory framework: V I P, R T. Validity. Integrity. Precision. Reliability. Timeliness. This is my memory aid, rather than a universal taxonomy. Deduplication can affect several dimensions. Today, the question is whether two records represent two entities, or one entity represented twice.

When a reliable unique identifier is unavailable, combine selected, standardized attributes into a composite key. Our teaching example uses date of birth, sex and district. First, standardize capitalization and formats. Then build the key and use it to form candidate groups. Select fields with stakeholders under an approved protocol. An identical key can occur for different people. A missing or changed district can also separate records that really belong together.

The workflow starts with co-design. Standardize the records. Build the key. Block records to avoid comparing every possible pair. Compare exact and, where appropriate, fuzzy fields. Flag candidates. Then adjudicate using permitted source information. A name similarity of ninety-six percent is not a ninety-six percent probability that two people are the same. Review can establish a confirmed duplicate, or separate people. Resolve only supported links and preserve an audit trail. A match is a flag, not a verdict.

There is no magic threshold. Lowering it can capture more duplicates while creating more false matches. Raising it can miss genuine duplicates. Validate precision and recall for your setting. Also measure what blocking missed. In our synthetic notebook, the key retains five hundred fifty-two of six hundred true duplicate pairs: ninety-two percent blocking recall. Candidate-pair recall alone hides those forty-eight missed pairs.

Reliable, governed unique identifiers can support exact matching. Composite-key and probabilistic approaches can generate candidates where reliable identifiers are unavailable. Neither national identifiers nor biometrics are infallible. Missing, erroneous or incorrectly linked identifiers still need quality checks. Real-world linkage requires an approved protocol, privacy safeguards, minimum-necessary information, secure processing, appropriate ethics and governance review, and documented adjudication.

The same question reaches far beyond health. A student transfers schools. A customer joins through two channels. A beneficiary appears on multiple assistance lists. One shopper creates several accounts. Government records span disconnected systems. In each case, decide what entity you are counting before you interpret the total.

Duplicates can change the weight that particular people receive in an analysis. That can shift distributions, coefficient estimates, uncertainty and model behavior.
